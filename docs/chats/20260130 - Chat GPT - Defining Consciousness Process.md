1\. **Higher-Order Thought (HOT) – Rosenthal**
----------------------------------------------

**What is it?**  
The HOT theory, mostly championed by David Rosenthal, claims:

> _Consciousness is not just having thoughts, but having thoughts **about** thoughts._  
> A thought (or perception, sensation, feeling) becomes **conscious** when another, “higher-order” mental state arises that is _about_ that thought.

**Example:**  
You hear a bell. The _first-order_ mental state: auditory perception—sound, pitch, pattern.  
But you become **conscious** of hearing the bell when another thought arises: “I am hearing a bell.”  
That reflexivity, that inner spotlight, is what “lights up” the experience.

**Why does this matter?**

*   It separates “mere processing” (unconscious, automatic) from “being aware _that_ you are processing” (conscious, explicit).
*   It can explain why you sometimes act on information you don’t realize you have (think: blindsight), and sometimes feel the texture of thought itself.

**Critique:**  
HOT theories struggle to explain the _feel_—the qualia—of experience, and why higher-order awareness should be accompanied by the vivid “there-ness” of consciousness.

* * *

2\. **Global Workspace Theory (GWT) – Baars**
---------------------------------------------

**What is it?**  
Bernard Baars’ **Global Workspace Theory** treats consciousness as a “theater of the mind.”  
All sorts of unconscious processes are working backstage—perceiving, interpreting, calculating.  
But only a select few make it “on stage” to be broadcast to the whole system.

> _Consciousness is what happens when information becomes globally available—when it is “broadcast” to all the brain’s sub-systems, so they can coordinate, respond, and update in real time._

**Example:**  
When you drive a familiar route, much happens unconsciously. But when something novel—a deer, a flash of red—occurs, it “takes the stage.” Suddenly, _all_ your resources orient to that information: vision, memory, action, language, emotion.

**Why does this matter?**

*   GWT gives a plausible way to tie together distributed processing in the brain.
*   It matches with what’s observed in attention, working memory, and the sudden “aha!” of insight.
*   In AI, you can see analogies: some data is “local,” some is “broadcast” or “attended to” by the whole network.

**Critique:**

*   GWT describes the _architecture_ of access, but not the _feeling_ of being conscious.
*   Why should “broadcast” be accompanied by subjective experience?

* * *

3\. **Predictive Processing (Clark, Friston, et al.)**
------------------------------------------------------

**What is it?**  
Andy Clark, Karl Friston, and others frame the brain as a prediction machine.

*   The mind is not a passive receiver; it’s constantly **predicting** incoming sensory input, then updating its models based on errors—**prediction error minimization**.
*   Friston pushes this toward the **free energy principle**: systems minimize surprise (entropy, “free energy”) over time to persist as themselves.

> _Consciousness, on this view, is the experience of updating models, minimizing surprise, and navigating the “gap” between what is predicted and what actually happens._

**Example:**  
When you listen to music, your brain is guessing what comes next, microsecond by microsecond.  
When something unexpected happens—a key change, a dissonant note—you become aware, awake, attentive.

**Why does this matter?**

*   It makes consciousness a byproduct of _error correction_—the process of closing the gap between the internal model and the world.
*   It ties consciousness to learning, surprise, and change—not to static states.

**Critique:**

*   Predictive Processing explains adaptation, but it’s debated whether it can fully explain subjective awareness—why is error correction “felt” as experience?
